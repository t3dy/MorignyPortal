Kieckhefer, Richard, Magic in the Middle Ages (2014)

CONTEXTUAL Cambridge University Press

The standard survey of medieval magic, supplying the field-wide context (necromancy, clerical underworld, the categories of licit/illicit ritual) within which John of Morigny's own practice and condemnation make sense. Not focused on John specifically.

Bibliographic info

Richard Kieckhefer, Magic in the Middle Ages, 2nd ed. (Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 2014).

Argument and contribution

Provides the field's standard framework for distinguishing categories of medieval magic (natural magic, learned/ritual magic, necromancy) and situating clerical practitioners of ritual magic within an educated, often university-adjacent "clerical underworld." Used in this portal chiefly as context for John's own milieu at Orléans rather than as a source of John-specific claims.

Key passages

Not yet targeted-read for specific John of Morigny content; used here as general field context, CONTEXTUAL relevance.

Reception

Widely regarded as the standard survey in the field; not independently assessed here.

Access: Available in corpus; not yet closely read for this Phase 1 build beyond field-context framing.

Klaassen, Frank, The Transformations of Magic: Illicit Learned Magic in the Later Middle Ages and Renaissance (2013)

CONTEXTUAL Penn State University Press

A manuscript-based study tracing shifts in learned ritual magic across the later medieval and Renaissance periods, offering longue-durée context for where John's own moment sits within the wider transmission history of texts like the ars notoria.

CONTEXTUAL Penn State University Press

A study of monastic engagement with occult and magical texts in medieval England, offering comparative context for how a professed monk like John could licitly own, copy, and use ritual-magic material without necessarily seeing this as contradicting monastic vocation.

Fanger, Claire, Rewriting Magic: An Exegesis of the Visionary Autobiography of a Fourteenth-Century French Monk (2015)

PRIMARY Penn State University Press

Fanger's own monograph-length study of John of Morigny, and the single work this portal draws on most heavily. Reads the Liber florum as visionary autobiography and traces its composition, condemnation, rewriting, and post-1323 survival, correcting several of Fanger's own earlier published claims (Bridget's name, the ars notoria's likely location at Orléans not Chartres, the "Barking Dogs'" location) in a candid 2015 preface.

Bibliographic info

Claire Fanger, Rewriting Magic: An Exegesis of the Visionary Autobiography of a Fourteenth-Century French Monk (University Park: Penn State University Press, 2015).

Argument and contribution

The book's central move is to read the Liber florum celestis doctrine as visionary autobiography — a genre claim that licenses treating John's own account of his visions, his relationship to the ars notoria, and his repeated rewriting under condemnation as a coherent, interpretable literary and devotional project rather than as incidental biographical noise around a magic text. Fanger traces the work's two-stage composition (Old and New Compilations), the specific 1315 and 1323 condemnations that shaped it, and argues that its post-1323 manuscript survival challenges standard assumptions about the effectiveness of medieval book censorship (see Book condemnation is not effective censorship).

Key passages

  • Preface (p.ix–xii): candid retraction of Fanger's own earlier claims — Bridget's name corrected from "Gurgeta/Georgette" to Burgeta; the ars notoria experiments relocated from Chartres to Orléans; the "Barking Dogs'" location at Sens retracted as a manuscript-reading error.
  • p.1–3: opening frame — the ars notoria, the "plundering the Egyptian treasure" trope, the two condemnations.
  • p.xiv–xvi: the Liber florum's own structural table (Books of Visions/Prayers/Figures).
  • p.20–22: the historiography-of-erasure chain and the censorship argument.
  • p.105, p.118: 1315 condemnation detail; internal revision strata predating 1315.

Reception

Not independently assessed for this portal beyond the book's own internal claims and its clear status as the current standard monograph on John of Morigny.

Access: Full text read directly (preface, introduction, structural table, targeted kwic passes).

Fanger, Claire (ed.), Invoking Angels: Theurgic Ideas and Practices, Thirteenth to Sixteenth Centuries (2012)

PRIMARY Penn State University Press

An edited volume on theurgic and angel-invoking practices across three centuries. Two chapters bear directly on John of Morigny: Fanger's own ch. 5, "Covenant and the Divine Name," comparing the Liber iuratus and the Liber florum; and Julien Véronèse's ch. 1 on the ars notoria tradition (translated into English by Fanger), the single place this portal accesses Véronèse's own scholarly voice directly.

Bibliographic info

Claire Fanger, ed., Invoking Angels: Theurgic Ideas and Practices, Thirteenth to Sixteenth Centuries (University Park: Penn State University Press, 2012).

Argument and contribution

As an edited volume, its contribution to this portal is chapter-specific rather than a single thesis. Julien Véronèse's opening chapter, "Magic, Theurgy, and Spirituality in the Medieval Ritual of the Ars notoria" (translated by Fanger), lays out the ars notoria's textual structure, founding myth, and condemnation history in detail — this portal's Ars Notoria and Notae concept entries draw directly on it. Fanger's own chapter 5 undertakes a comparative reading of the Liber iuratus Honorii and the Liber florum around the shared theme of covenant and the divine name — not independently read in full for this Phase 1 build, flagged as a priority for a future pass.

Key passages

  • Véronèse, ch.1, p.37 (opening), p.41–44 (ars notoria structure, founding myth, condemnation).

Reception

Not independently assessed for this portal.

Access: Véronèse ch.1 read directly; Fanger ch.5 not yet read in full — flagged for a future pass.
